You're standing on a balcony. High up. And out of nowhere, a voice in your head says: jump. You don't want to die. You're not even sad. So you step back, heart racing, and you never tell anyone. The French have a name for it. The call of the void. And here's the part nobody believes. In 2012, researchers asked four hundred and thirty-one students about it. Among the ones who had never once thought about ending their life, about half had felt that urge. So where does it come from? The researchers think it works like this. Your brain spots the danger before you do. In a split second, it pulls you back from the edge. Then the slower, thinking part of you catches up. It looks around. The railing is solid. You were never falling. So it asks: why did I just step back? And it guesses wrong. It decides: I must have wanted to jump. That voice was never telling you to jump. It was your brain explaining why it had already saved you. The urge to jump is really the urge to live.
