# Why the least skilled feel the most confident (Full Blend, v1, approved)

In 1995, a man in Pittsburgh robbed two banks in broad daylight, with no mask. He wasn't worried, because he had rubbed lemon juice on his face. Lemon juice can be used as invisible ink, so he believed it would make his face invisible to the cameras. He was caught soon after. Remember when someone at school or work was completely sure they were right, and completely wrong? And you see, that robbery inspired two psychologists at Cornell University, David Dunning and Justin Kruger. In 1999 they tested students on logic, grammar and humour, then asked them how well they thought they had done. The students who scored near the bottom, around the twelfth percentile, guessed they were around the sixty-second, better than most of the class. The skills you need to do something well are the same skills you need to notice you're doing it badly. Psychologists named it the Dunning-Kruger effect. But here's the twist: the best students did the opposite. They underestimated themselves, because they assumed what felt easy to them was easy for everyone. So the most confident person in the room isn't always the one who knows the most. And if you ever doubt yourself, that might be a sign you know more than you think.
