# Why do you check the door is locked, and then feel less sure?

Script style: Full Blend without the star step (no famous person's exact words could be verified for this topic, so
none is used). You First opening, Story Flow science, "But here's the twist", close on the viewer. Draft for approval.

## Script (~200 words, about 63 s)

You lock the door. You walk away. And three steps later, a voice in your head says: did you really lock it?
So you go back. It's locked. You walk away again. And somehow, you feel even less sure than before.

Now, in 2003, two Dutch psychologists, Marcel van den Hout and Merel Kindt, wanted to know why, so they sat ordinary
people in front of a gas stove on a computer screen and asked them to turn it off and check it, again and again,
twenty times. And you see, when they tested them afterwards, their memory was just as accurate as everyone else's,
they still knew the stove was off. But here's the twist: they no longer believed it, because the memory of that last
check had gone blurry, with less detail and less colour, so they trusted it less. And three years later, another team
tried it with a real kitchen stove, and the same thing happened.

So the more you check, the less you trust your own memory.
Next time, check once. Look at it properly. Then walk away, because going back won't make you surer. It will make you
less sure.

## Facts and where each comes from
- van den Hout, M., & Kindt, M. (2003). Repeated checking causes memory distrust. Behaviour Research and Therapy, 41(3),
  301-316. Healthy participants (39 and 40 in two experiments) checked a virtual gas stove for 20 trials; a control
  group checked virtual light bulbs. Memory accuracy was the same in both groups; confidence, vividness and detail fell
  only in the group that kept checking the stove.
- Radomsky, A. S., Gilchrist, P. T., & Dussault, D. (2006). Repeated checking really does cause memory distrust.
  Behaviour Research and Therapy. 50 students, a real kitchen stove: reduced memory confidence, vividness and detail.
- No famous person is quoted: reports say Jessica Alba has spoken about re-checking doors, but her exact words could not
  be confirmed, so she is left out.
- The closing advice follows from the finding (re-checking lowers confidence); it is not a treatment for OCD, and the
  video does not mention OCD.
