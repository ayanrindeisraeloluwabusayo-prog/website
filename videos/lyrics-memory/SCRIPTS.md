# Why do you remember song lyrics from years ago, but not what you read yesterday?

Two versions of the same video for the owner's comparison. Same facts, pictures and 3D shots; only the script
and pacing differ. Voice: Kokoro am_michael at speed 1.25 (the owner's "mix"), free.

## Version A: punchy (short lines, small pauses)
Why do you remember a song from ten years ago, but not what you read yesterday?
You never studied that song. You never even tried.
But every word is still there.
It isn't your memory. It's the melody.
In 1994, a researcher had people learn the words of a song. Some heard them sung. Some heard them spoken.
Sung once, the melody didn't help.
But sung again and again, with the same tune? The singers won.
Because a tune is a track. Each word has one place to go. The rhythm tells you how long it is. The rhyme tells you how it ends.
And you didn't hear that song once. You heard it a hundred times.
What you read yesterday? Once. No tune. No rhyme. No repeat.
Here's the part that gets me.
In 2015, scientists found where the brain keeps the songs you've known for years.
It's one of the last places Alzheimer's reaches.
People who forget their own children's names can still sing every word.
So that song isn't stuck in your head. It's one of the safest things in it.

## Version B: story flow (starts in the middle, no dead pauses)
A song comes on that you haven't heard in ten years, and before you even think about it, you're singing every single word. But ask you what you read yesterday, and it's gone. You see, you never studied that song, and that's the strange part, because in 1994 a researcher tested exactly this. She had people learn the words of a song, some hearing them sung and some hearing them spoken. Sung just once, the melody didn't help at all. But when the same tune repeated, verse after verse, the people who heard it sung remembered more. That's because a tune works like a track, every word has exactly one place to sit, the rhythm tells you how long it is, and the rhyme tells you how it ends. And you didn't hear that song once, you heard it a hundred times, while yesterday's reading got one quiet pass with no tune and no repeat. Now here's the part that gets me. In 2015, scientists went looking for where the brain keeps songs you've known for years, and they found it's one of the last places Alzheimer's disease reaches. Which is why people who can't remember their own children's names can still sing every word. So that song isn't stuck in your head. It's one of the safest things in it.

## Facts and sources
- Wallace, W. T. (1994), "Memory for music: Effect of melody on recall of text", Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 20, 1471-1485. Unfamiliar ballads; text recalled better when sung
  with the same melody repeated across verses; with a single verse (heard once) spoken text was recalled better.
  Later work (Kilgour et al. 2000) suggests slower presentation rate explains part of the benefit: we say
  "remembered more", not why in detail.
- Jacobsen et al. (2015), "Why musical memory can be preserved in advanced Alzheimer's disease", Brain. The regions
  that encode long-known music (caudal anterior cingulate, ventral pre-SMA) show among the least atrophy and
  metabolic disruption in Alzheimer's. "One of the last places Alzheimer's reaches" is the plain-words version.
- "Can still sing every word" describes the well-documented preserved musical memory in many patients, not all:
  on screen we add "many".
- "A hundred times" is a figure of speech for heavy repetition, not a measured number.
