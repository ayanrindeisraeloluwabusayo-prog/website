# Why ₦9,999 feels cheaper than ₦10,000 (Full Blend draft)

You're in the shop. One pair of shoes is ten thousand naira. The other is nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine. And somehow, the second one feels like a bargain. It's one naira cheaper. One. Now listen to this. When Steve Jobs opened the iTunes music store in 2003, he didn't price a song at one dollar. He priced every song at ninety-nine cents. Now, scientists have a name for this, the left-digit effect, and in 2005 two researchers, Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz, showed why it works: your brain reads a price from left to right, and it judges the size by the first digit, before it has even finished reading. So a nine at the front feels like nine, not ten. That's why, when you walk into a mall, you almost never see a round price. You see nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine, or one thousand, two hundred and forty-five. And that's no accident, because in 2010, Manoj Thomas and his colleagues found that an exact-looking price like that feels smaller than a round one, even when they're almost the same. And you see, it changes what people buy, because when two marketing professors tested prices ending in nine in a real clothing catalogue, the nine-ending prices sold more in every single test they ran. But here's the twist: it doesn't only work on cheap things. In 2012, economists looked at more than twenty-two million used car sales and found that prices dropped sharply every time the mileage crossed a round number, like going from 79,900 to 80,000. A few extra miles, and buyers paid much less, just because the first digit changed. So Steve Jobs knew it, and your local shop knows it too. Next time you see a price ending in nine, round it up in your head. Nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine is ten thousand naira.
