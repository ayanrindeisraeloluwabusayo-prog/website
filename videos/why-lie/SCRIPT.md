# Why people lie, and who does most of it (Full Blend, v1, waiting for approval)

Someone asks how you are. You say "fine". You're not. That's a lie. Remember when Lance Armstrong sat down with Oprah Winfrey in 2013 and admitted that his seven Tour de France wins were built on doping, calling it "one big lie"? For years, he'd looked the world in the eye and denied it. And you see, most of us lie too, just smaller. In 1996, psychologist Bella DePaulo had people keep a diary of every conversation for a week. College students told about two lies a day. Other adults told about one. Most lies weren't meant to hurt anyone. People lied to look better, feel better, or spare someone's feelings. So who lies the most? A 2015 study of about a thousand people, aged six to seventy-seven, found that lying peaks in the teenage years, then drops as we get older. But here's the twist: most people hardly lie at all. In 2010, researchers asked a thousand Americans how many lies they'd told in the last day. Six in ten said none. And half of all the lies came from just five percent of people. A small group of prolific liars does most of the lying for everyone. So if it feels like everyone lies, it's really a few people lying a lot. Next time someone tells you "everybody lies", you can tell them: most people don't.

## Sources (checked)
- Lance Armstrong, interview with Oprah Winfrey (taped 14 Jan 2013, aired 17-18 Jan): admitted banned substances and blood transfusions in all seven Tour de France wins and called it "one big lie" (UPI, MPR, Al Jazeera).
- DePaulo, Kashy, Kirkendol, Wyer & Epstein, "Lying in everyday life", JPSP 70(5) (1996): week-long diaries; 77 college students averaged about 2 lies a day (1.96), 70 community members about 1 (0.97); most lies were self-centred (to look or feel better), many to spare others.
- Debey, De Schryver, Logan, Suchotzki & Verschuere, "From junior to senior Pinocchio", Acta Psychologica 160:58-68 (2015): 1,005 people aged 6-77; lying frequency rose in childhood, peaked in adolescence, and declined in adulthood.
- Serota, Levine & Boster, "The prevalence of lying in America: Three studies of self-reported lies", Human Communication Research 36(1) (2010): 1,000 US adults; 59.9% reported no lies in the past 24 hours; half of all reported lies were told by 5.3% of people.
