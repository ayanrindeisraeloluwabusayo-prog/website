# The halo effect (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

Someone walks in looking sharp. Nice clothes, great smile. And before they've said a word, you've already decided they're smart, kind and good at their job. Remember when someone at school or work was good-looking, and somehow everyone assumed they were good at everything else too? In 1920, a psychologist called Edward Thorndike asked army officers to rate their soldiers on things like intelligence, leadership and physique. The scores stuck together far more than they should have. Soldiers rated as good-looking and well-built were also rated smarter and better leaders. He called it the halo effect: one good trait shines over everything else, like a halo. And you see, in 1977, psychologists at the University of Michigan showed students a video of a teacher with a strong foreign accent. Half saw him being warm and friendly. Half saw the same man being cold and strict. The students who saw the warm version found his looks, his mannerisms, even his accent appealing. The students who saw the cold version called the very same things irritating. But here's the twist: when the researchers asked them, the students insisted that liking him had nothing to do with it. Some even said it worked the other way round, that his accent was the reason they didn't like him. So the halo isn't something you choose. It works on you without you noticing. Next time you meet someone, ask yourself: do I really know they're good, or do they just look like they are?

(About 250 words, about 80-85 seconds.)

## Sources (checked)
- Edward L. Thorndike, "A constant error in psychological ratings", Journal of Applied Psychology 4(1):25-29 (1920): officers rated soldiers on physique, intelligence, leadership and character; ratings correlated more than they should (e.g. physique with intelligence about .31, with leadership about .39); Thorndike named it the "halo".
- Nisbett & Wilson, "The halo effect: Evidence for unconscious alteration of judgments", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 35(4):250-256 (1977), University of Michigan: 118 students; videotaped Belgian instructor with a pronounced accent, warm vs cold; warm viewers rated his appearance, mannerisms and accent appealing, cold viewers irritating; students denied that liking affected their ratings, and cold viewers believed the influence ran the opposite way.
