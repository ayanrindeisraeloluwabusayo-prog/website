# The dress: blue and black, or white and gold? (Full Blend, v1, waiting for approval)

Look at this dress. Is it blue and black, or white and gold? In February 2015, one blurry photo split the whole internet in half. Remember when Taylor Swift tweeted, "I don't understand this odd dress debate and I feel like it's a trick somehow. I'm confused and scared. PS it's obviously blue and black"? Kim Kardashian saw white and gold. Kanye West saw blue and black. And you see, the dress really was blue and black. So why did so many people see something else? Scientists at Wellesley College surveyed about fourteen hundred people. Fifty-seven percent saw blue and black. Thirty percent saw white and gold. The rest saw something in between. The answer is light. Your brain never just sees colour. It guesses what the light is like, then takes it away. If your brain decided the dress was in bluish shadow, it took away the blue, and you saw white and gold. If it decided the dress was under warm, yellow light, it took away the yellow, and you saw blue and black. But here's the twist: your sleep schedule might decide which one you see. In 2017, Pascal Wallisch at New York University studied more than thirteen thousand people. Early risers, who spend more time in daylight, were more likely to see white and gold. Night owls, who spend more time under warm indoor light, leaned blue and black. So nobody was lying, and nobody was wrong. Your brain filled the gap with your own life. Next time you argue about a colour with a friend, ask them one thing first: what time do you wake up?

- Wallisch, "Illumination assumptions account for individual differences in the perceptual interpretation of a profoundly ambiguous stimulus in the color domain: 'The dress'", Journal of Vision 17(4):5 (2017), NYU: 13,417 people; assuming shadow went with white/gold; early risers (larks) more often saw white and gold, night owls slightly more often blue and black; explained by typical light exposure.
