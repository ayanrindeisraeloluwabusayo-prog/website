More than two thousand years ago, a book of questions credited to Aristotle asked: why do people yawn when they see others yawn? Now, in the 1980s, a psychologist called Robert Provine sat students in front of a screen full of yawning faces, and you see, more than half of them yawned within five minutes. And it didn't even need a face, because Provine found that just reading about yawning, or even thinking about it, was enough to set people off. But here's the twist: in 2011, two Italian researchers, Ivan Norscia and Elisabetta Palagi, watched more than a hundred people in everyday life for a whole year, and the yawns didn't spread equally. They jumped most often, and fastest, to family, then friends, then people they barely knew, and least of all to strangers. So Aristotle's question still has no perfect answer, but there's one clue: a yawn travels along the people you care about. Next time you yawn, watch who catches it. It's usually the people closest to you.
