Watch a man's lips say "ga". Now play the sound "ba" over the top. What do you hear? Most people hear a third sound, "da", a sound nobody made. Remember when the internet split in 2018 over a four-second clip that said "Yanny" or "Laurel"? Ellen DeGeneres heard "Laurel". The singer Yanni heard "Yanny". Our ears aren't as sure as we think. And you see, scientists found this one by accident. In the 1970s, at the University of Surrey in England, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald asked a technician to dub one sound onto a video of a different sound. When they played it back, they heard something that wasn't there. In 1976, they published it as "Hearing lips and seeing voices". Your brain doesn't wait for your ears. It mixes what you hear with what you see, and when the two disagree, it makes a best guess. That's why it's easier to follow someone in a noisy room when you can see their face. And even when you know the trick, it still works. Close your eyes, and you hear "ba". Open them, and it's "da" again. But here's the twist: not everyone falls for it. In 2015, a team at Baylor College of Medicine tested more than a hundred people. Some heard the made-up sound every single time. Others never heard it at all. And a year later, each person's score had barely changed. So next time you're sure you heard something, ask yourself: did you hear it, or did you see it?
