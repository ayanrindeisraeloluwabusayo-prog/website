Someone smiles at you. Big smile, teeth and all. But something feels off. Is it real? Remember when Tyra Banks told the models on America's Next Top Model to "smize", to smile with their eyes? She was onto something. And you see, back in the 1860s, a French doctor, Guillaume Duchenne, used electric currents to move the muscles of people's faces. He noticed that a real smile of joy also uses a muscle around the eyes, the one that makes them crinkle, and he believed most people can't move it on purpose. Scientists still call a smile with crinkled eyes a Duchenne smile, and for a long time it was treated as the sign of a real one. But here's the twist: the eyes can fake it too. In a 2013 study at Northeastern University in Boston, people were asked to smile on purpose, and about seven in ten of those put-on smiles had the eye crinkle anyway. So the better clue may be timing. Researchers have found that put-on smiles tend to switch on faster and drop away more suddenly, while a real smile builds and fades slowly. So don't just watch the eyes. Watch how a smile leaves the face. Even Tyra's smize can be practised. Next time someone smiles at you, notice how slowly it fades.
