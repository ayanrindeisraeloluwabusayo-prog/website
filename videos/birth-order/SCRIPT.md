# Does being the oldest, middle or youngest child shape you? (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

The oldest is the bossy, responsible one. The middle child is the peacemaker nobody notices. The youngest is the spoiled, funny rebel. Almost everyone believes it. Remember when someone guessed your birth order just from your personality, and felt clever about it? And you see, psychologists have argued about this for about a hundred years, ever since Alfred Adler said birth order shapes who we become. So in 2015, researchers in Germany tested it properly. They looked at more than twenty thousand adults from Germany, the US and Britain, and compared brothers and sisters within the same families. Outgoing or shy, calm or anxious, kind, organised, imaginative: on every one of these personality traits, birth order made no real difference. The only thing they found was tiny: firstborns scored very slightly higher on intelligence, and saw themselves as a little more intellectual. But here's the twist: if it isn't real, why does it feel so true? Because when we compare siblings, we're comparing people of different ages. Of course the oldest seems more mature. They are older. The stereotype is built on age, not birth order. So the next time someone says "you're such a middle child", you can tell them science checked. Who you are isn't decided by when you arrived.

## Sources (checked)
- Alfred Adler proposed birth-order effects on personality in the early 20th century.
- Rohrer, Egloff & Schmukle, "Examining the effects of birth order on personality", PNAS 112(46):14224-14229 (2015), Universities of Leipzig and Mainz: 20,000+ adults from Germany, the USA and Great Britain, between- and within-family designs; no birth-order effects on extraversion, emotional stability, agreeableness, conscientiousness or imagination; a small decline in measured intelligence and about a tenth of a standard deviation in self-reported intellect with later birth position.
- The age explanation: within-family comparisons and parents' impressions compare siblings of different ages, which earlier studies could not separate from birth order (as the 2015 authors and later commentaries note).
