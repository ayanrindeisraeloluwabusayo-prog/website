# Why does the journey back home always feel shorter? (draft 1, for the owner's approval)

Format: blue explainer edit + 3D glass head (option B). Voice: Brian (ElevenLabs), natural pace. About 50 s.

## Script (about 150 words)
Why does the journey back home always feel shorter?
Same road. Same distance.
But going, it felt like forever. Coming back, you blinked, and you were home.
It isn't the road. It's your brain.
In 2011, researchers in the Netherlands asked people about trips they made both ways.
On average, the trip back felt about twenty-two percent shorter.
They even sent people home on a different road, of the same length. It still felt shorter.
So it's not because you know the way.
Here's what's happening.
On the way there, the trip took longer than you expected.
So your brain prepares for the trip back to be long too.
And when it isn't as long as you feared, it feels short.
Your brain doesn't just count the minutes. It compares them to what it expected.
And a 2015 study found something stranger. While you're on the road, both trips feel about the same.
The difference only shows up when you look back.
So next time you dread a long journey, expect it to be long.
It just might fly by.

## Facts and sources
- van de Ven, van Rijswijk & Roy (2011), "The return trip effect: Why the return trip often seems to take less
  time", Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 18(5), 827-832. Bus, bike and video studies; the effect held on a
  different route of equal length (so not familiarity); explained by violated expectations. The "about 22%
  shorter" figure comes from their survey (press release), so it is said as "on average... felt about".
- Ozawa et al. (2015), PLOS ONE, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0127779: the effect appears when people judge the trip
  afterwards, not during it.
