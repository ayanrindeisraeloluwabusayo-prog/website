# Why you get déjà vu (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

You walk into a room for the first time. Someone laughs, a door closes, and suddenly you freeze. You've lived this exact moment before. Remember when The Matrix gave us an answer? Neo sees the same black cat walk past twice, and Trinity tells him: "A déjà vu is usually a glitch in the Matrix." Now, about six in ten people say they've had déjà vu, young people most of all. So in 2012, psychologist Anne Cleary at Colorado State University set out to make it happen in a lab. Her team built scenes in the video game The Sims and showed them to people one by one. And you see, some of the new scenes were secretly laid out just like earlier ones: the same shape, things in the same places, but every object different. When people couldn't remember the earlier scene, those copycat layouts were the ones that gave them déjà vu. Your brain spots the match, but it can't find the memory it came from, so all you're left with is the feeling: I've been here before. But here's the twist: déjà vu often comes with a feeling that you know what happens next. In 2018, Cleary tested that too. In the middle of déjà vu, people felt sure which way the path would turn. They were right no more often than a random guess. The feeling of knowing was real. The knowing wasn't. So déjà vu isn't a glitch in the Matrix. It's your memory finding a match it can't name. Next time it happens, look around and ask yourself: where have I seen a place shaped like this before?

## Sources (checked)
- The Matrix (1999): Neo sees a black cat pass twice; Trinity: "A déjà vu is usually a glitch in the Matrix. It happens when
  they change something." (IMDb quotes). Named as plain text only, no film footage or stills.
- Brown, "A review of the déjà vu experience", Psychological Bulletin 129(3):394-413 (2003): about 60% of people report
  déjà vu; it becomes less frequent with age.
- Cleary et al., "Familiarity from the configuration of objects in 3-dimensional space and its relation to déjà vu: A virtual
  reality investigation", Consciousness and Cognition 21:969-975 (2012), Colorado State University. Scenes built in The Sims;
  new scenes whose spatial layout matched an earlier scene raised familiarity and déjà vu reports, when people could not
  recall the earlier scene.
- Cleary & Claxton, "Déjà vu: An illusion of prediction", Psychological Science 29(4):635-644 (2018). During déjà vu, people
  felt more sure of the next turn in a path but were no better than chance at predicting it.
