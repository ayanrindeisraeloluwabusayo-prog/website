# Déjà vu: "I've lived this moment before" (Full Blend, v1, waiting for approval)

You walk into a room you've never been in. Someone says something. And suddenly you're sure you've lived this exact moment before: the same words, the same light, like your life just replayed. Remember when, in The Matrix, Neo sees the same black cat walk past twice, and Trinity tells him déjà vu is a glitch in the Matrix? Well, it is a glitch, but not in reality. It's a glitch in your memory. About two in three people say they've felt it, and it happens most between the ages of fifteen and twenty-five. And you see, in 2018, a psychologist at Colorado State University, Anne Cleary, built rooms inside a video game. Some of the new rooms had the same layout as rooms people had already walked through, rooms they didn't remember. Those rooms made déjà vu more likely. Your brain spotted a familiar pattern, but couldn't find where it came from. But here's the twist: during déjà vu, people felt they knew which way to turn next, like they could see the future. They couldn't. They were right no more often than a guess. And when scientists in Scotland scanned people's brains during déjà vu, the busiest parts weren't the memory areas. They were the parts that check for mistakes. So déjà vu isn't your brain breaking down. It's your brain noticing something feels familiar, and checking it. Next time it happens, don't panic. It means your memory's fact-checker is doing its job.

## Sources (checked)
- The Matrix (1999): Neo sees a black cat pass twice, says "déjà vu"; Trinity: "A déjà vu is usually a glitch in the Matrix." Text reference only, no footage or art.
- Alan S. Brown (SMU): about two-thirds of people report at least one déjà vu; most common at ages 15-25, falls with age ("The Déjà Vu Illusion"; his 2003 Psychological Bulletin review puts it at about 60%).
- Cleary & Claxton, "Déjà vu: an illusion of prediction", Psychological Science 29:635-644 (2018), Colorado State University: virtual scenes built in The Sims; new scenes with the same layout as an earlier, unrecalled scene raised déjà vu reports; people felt they knew the next turn but predicted at chance.
- Urquhart, Sivakumaran, Macfarlane & O'Connor (University of St Andrews), fMRI of experimentally induced déjà vu (reported 2016, published in Memory): conflict and decision regions (anterior cingulate, medial prefrontal, parietal) were active, not the hippocampus; framed as the brain checking for memory errors.
