You walk into a room you've never been in. Someone says something. And suddenly you're sure you've lived this exact moment before: the same words, the same light, like your life just replayed. Remember when, in The Matrix, Neo sees the same black cat walk past twice, and Trinity tells him deja vu is a glitch in the Matrix? Well, it is a glitch, but not in reality. It's a glitch in your memory. About two in three people say they've felt it, and it happens most between the ages of fifteen and twenty-five. And you see, in 2018, a psychologist at Colorado State University, Anne Cleary, built rooms inside a video game. Some of the new rooms had the same layout as rooms people had already walked through, rooms they didn't remember. Those rooms made deja vu more likely. Your brain spotted a familiar pattern, but couldn't find where it came from. But here's the twist: during deja vu, people felt they knew which way to turn next, like they could see the future. They couldn't. They were right no more often than a guess. And when scientists in Scotland scanned people's brains during deja vu, the busiest parts weren't the memory areas. They were the parts that check for mistakes. So deja vu isn't your brain breaking down. It's your brain noticing something feels familiar, and checking it. Next time it happens, don't panic. It means your memory's fact-checker is doing its job.
