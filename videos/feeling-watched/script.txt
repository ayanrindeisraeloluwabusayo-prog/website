You're walking home at night. The street is empty. And suddenly the back of your neck goes cold, like someone is watching you. Remember when you turned around, sure someone was staring, and nobody was there? And you see, in 2013, scientists in London and Sydney showed people faces on a screen, then blurred the eyes so it was hard to tell where they were looking. When people couldn't tell, they didn't guess fifty-fifty. Every person in the study leaned the same way: that face is looking at me. The researchers called it an expectation that gaze is directed toward us. Your brain doesn't wait for proof. When the light is low, or someone is wearing sunglasses, it fills the gap with its favourite answer: you're being watched. But here's the twist: this isn't your brain being broken. It's your brain being careful. As one of the researchers, Colin Clifford, explained, a direct stare can be a sign of a threat, and if something might be a threat, you don't want to miss it. Missing a real stare could cost you. Turning around for nothing costs you one second. And babies already prefer faces that look straight at them, so this may be built in from the start. So that feeling on the back of your neck isn't proof that someone is there. It's your brain choosing safe over sorry. Next time you feel watched in the dark, turn on a light, and let your eyes give your brain the answer.
