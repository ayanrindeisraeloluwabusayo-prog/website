It's late. You're tired. Your eyes are burning. And you're still lying in bed, scrolling, telling yourself: just one more video. Remember when the boss of Netflix said his company's biggest rival wasn't another app? In 2017, Reed Hastings told investors, "we're competing with sleep." And you see, three years before that, psychologists in the Netherlands gave this habit a name: bedtime procrastination. Going to bed later than you planned, with nothing stopping you but you. In their survey of a hundred and seventy-seven people, the ones who struggled most with self-control put off bed the most, and got less sleep. Then people online gave it a sharper name: revenge bedtime procrastination. Because when your whole day belongs to work, school and everybody else, those late-night minutes feel like the only time that's really yours, so you hold on to them. But here's the twist: even when you finally put the phone down, sleep doesn't come. In 2017, a study of four hundred and twenty-three young adults found that the more often people binge-watched, the worse they slept and the more tired they felt. Ordinary TV watching didn't do that. Their brains were still switched on, still inside the story, and needed longer to cool down. So Netflix was right. It is competing with your sleep. Next time, take your me-time earlier in the evening, and put your phone out of reach of your bed.
