# Why you stay up late on your phone (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

It's late. You're tired. Your eyes are burning. And you're still lying in bed, scrolling, telling yourself: just one more video. Remember when the boss of Netflix said his company's biggest rival wasn't another app? In 2017, Reed Hastings told investors, "we're competing with sleep." And you see, three years before that, psychologists in the Netherlands gave this habit a name: bedtime procrastination. Going to bed later than you planned, with nothing stopping you but you. In their survey of 177 people, the ones who struggled most with self-control put off bed the most, and got less sleep. Then people online gave it a sharper name: revenge bedtime procrastination. Because when your whole day belongs to work, school and everybody else, those late-night minutes feel like the only time that's really yours, so you hold on to them. But here's the twist: even when you finally put the phone down, sleep doesn't come. In 2017, a study of 423 young adults found that the more often people binge-watched, the worse they slept and the more tired they felt. Ordinary TV watching didn't do that. Their brains were still switched on, still inside the story, and needed longer to cool down. So Netflix was right. It is competing with your sleep. Next time, take your me-time earlier in the evening, and put your phone out of reach of your bed.

(About 250 words, about 85-90 seconds.)

## Sources (checked)
- Reed Hastings, Netflix Q1 2017 earnings interview (April 2017): "we're competing with sleep, on the margin." The often-quoted ending "...and we're winning" could not be confirmed, so it is left out.
- Kroese, Evers, Adriaanse & de Ridder, "Bedtime procrastination: introducing a new area of procrastination", Frontiers in Psychology 5:611 (2014), Utrecht University. Online survey of 177 people: weaker self-regulation went with more bedtime procrastination, which predicted insufficient sleep.
- "Revenge bedtime procrastination": a later popular term from a Chinese expression about long working hours, spread in English by journalist Daphne K. Lee in 2020.
- Exelmans & Van den Bulck, "Binge viewing, sleep, and the role of pre-sleep arousal", Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine 13(8):1001-1008 (2017). 423 adults aged 18-25: more frequent binge-watching was linked to poorer sleep quality, more fatigue and more insomnia symptoms; regular TV viewing was not. Explained by pre-sleep arousal (the mind taking longer to wind down). Survey study, so it shows a link, not proof of cause.
