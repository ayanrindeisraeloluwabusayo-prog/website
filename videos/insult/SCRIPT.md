# Why one insult beats ten compliments (Full Blend)

Ten people tell you your outfit looks great. One person says it looks weird. Guess which one you're still thinking about at midnight? Remember Jimmy Kimmel's Mean Tweets? Famous, rich, successful people sat in front of a camera and read the nastiest things strangers had written about them. These people have millions of fans. But it's the one mean tweet that makes them flinch. Now, psychologists have a name for this, the negativity bias, and in 2001 Roy Baumeister and his team looked across hundreds of studies and reached one simple conclusion: bad is stronger than good. And you see, it even shows up with money, because economists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that losing a hundred dollars hurts about twice as much as winning a hundred dollars feels good. Scientists think your brain is built to notice the bad first, because for our ancestors, missing one danger mattered more than missing one nice thing. But here's the twist: good can still win. It just needs backup. Psychologist John Gottman studied couples for decades, and found that the ones who stayed together had about five good moments for every bad one, even during arguments. So one insult doesn't really beat ten compliments. It just shouts louder. Next time one comment ruins your day, count the good ones out loud. And when you have to criticise someone you love, make sure they've heard five good things from you first.
