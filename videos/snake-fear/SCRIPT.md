# Are we born scared of snakes and spiders? (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

There's a spider on the wall. Your body freezes before you even think. Remember when Indiana Jones looked down into a pit and said, "Snakes. Why'd it have to be snakes?" A hero who wasn't afraid of anything else. And you see, millions of us feel the same. So are we born with it? In 2017, researchers at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig showed six-month-old babies pictures of spiders and snakes, and of flowers and fish. The babies' pupils got bigger for the spiders and snakes, a sign their brains were on alert. But here's the twist: babies aren't actually afraid. In studies by Judy DeLoache and Vanessa LoBue, babies watched videos of snakes while hearing a happy or a frightened voice. They looked longer at the snakes when the voice sounded frightened, but they showed no fear themselves. What we're born with isn't the fear. It's a fast lane for learning it. Monkeys raised in a lab weren't scared of snakes at all, until they watched another monkey panic at one. Then they picked up the fear fast. But when a video was edited to make the monkey seem to panic at flowers, they never learned to fear flowers. So if you're scared of spiders, your brain isn't broken. It was built to learn that fear quickly. Next time you see a spider, stay calm. Someone small might be watching you.

## Sources (checked)
- Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981): Indiana Jones, "Snakes. Why'd it have to be snakes?"
- Hoehl, Hellmer, Johansson & Gredebäck, "Itsy Bitsy Spider…: Infants react with increased arousal to spiders and snakes", Frontiers in Psychology (Oct 2017), Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, Leipzig (with Vienna and Uppsala): 6-month-olds showed bigger pupil dilation to spiders and snakes than to flowers and fish. (A 2018 commentary questioned the interpretation, hence "on alert", not "afraid".)
- DeLoache & LoBue (2009), Developmental Science: 7-18-month-olds looked longer at snake videos with a fearful voice than a happy one, but showed no fear themselves (press release: "People aren't born afraid of spiders and snakes: fear is quickly learned during infancy", APS).
- Mineka et al. (1984) and Cook & Mineka (1989), Journal of Abnormal Psychology: lab-reared rhesus monkeys not initially afraid of snakes quickly acquired snake fear by watching fearful monkeys; with spliced videos they learned fear of toy snakes but not of flowers.
