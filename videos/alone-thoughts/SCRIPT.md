# People would rather shock themselves than sit alone with their thoughts (Full Blend, v1, waiting for approval)

You're in a waiting room. No phone, no music, nothing to do. Just you and your thoughts. How long before you reach for your phone? Remember when your phone died in a long queue, and the minutes felt like hours? In 2014, psychologists at the University of Virginia, led by Timothy Wilson, left people alone in a plain room for six to fifteen minutes with one simple job: think. No phone, no book, nothing. Most of them didn't enjoy it, and many found it hard to focus. So the researchers made it stranger. And you see, in one study they first gave people a small electric shock, and most said they would pay money never to feel it again. Then they left them alone for fifteen minutes with a button that would shock them. Two out of three men and one in four women pressed it anyway. One man pressed it a hundred and ninety times. But here's the twist: they didn't press it because they liked pain. They pressed it because doing something, anything, felt better than doing nothing. Even a shock was something to do. So when you grab your phone the second you're alone, it may not be the phone you're chasing. It's an escape from sitting with your own mind. Next time you're waiting, try one minute. No phone. Just you. See how long you last.

## Sources (checked)
- Wilson, Reinhard, Westgate et al., "Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind", Science 345(6192):75-77 (2014), University of Virginia: 11 studies; 6-15 minutes alone; most did not enjoy it and found it hard to concentrate; in the shock study, 12 of 18 men (67%) and 6 of 24 women (25%) shocked themselves at least once among those who had said they would pay to avoid the shock; one man shocked himself 190 times (an outlier left out of the analysis). The authors' reading: people prefer doing something, even something unpleasant, to doing nothing.
