# Why you can't remember being a baby (Full Blend, v1, waiting for approval)

Think of your very first memory. Got it? Now, how old were you? For most people, it's around three or four. You spent years learning to walk, to talk, to know your mum's face, and almost none of it is left. Remember when someone told you they remember their first birthday? In 2018, psychologists in London asked more than six thousand people for their earliest memory. Nearly four in ten said it came from age two or younger, and the researchers say most of those are probably made up, pieced together from photos and stories you heard later. And you see, scientists at Emory University recorded mothers talking with their three-year-olds about things they had done together. When those children were five, six and seven, they still remembered more than sixty percent of those moments. But from around seven, the forgetting speeds up, and those early memories start to fade. But here's the twist: babies do make memories. In 2025, scientists at Yale scanned the brains of babies as young as four months old while they looked at pictures. The brain's memory centre, the hippocampus, was already storing what they saw. So your baby memories were made. They may still be in there somewhere, behind a door you can't open anymore. So the next time someone says they remember being a baby, smile. That memory was real once. You just can't reach it.

## Sources (checked)
- Typical earliest memory around age 3-4 (childhood amnesia; e.g. Bauer & Larkina; Akhtar et al. report the average first memory at about 3.2 years).
- Akhtar, Justice, Morrison & Conway, "Fictional first memories", Psychological Science 29(10):1612-1619 (2018), City, University of London: 6,641 people; about 4 in 10 (38.6%) gave a first memory from age 2 or younger; the authors argue these are probably fictional, built from fragments, photos and family stories.
- Bauer & Larkina, "The onset of childhood amnesia in childhood", Memory (2014), Emory University: mothers talked with 3-year-olds about past events; at ages 5-7 children recalled over 60% of the events; adult-like forgetting sets in around age 7.
- Yates, Fel, Choi, Trach, Behm, Ellis & Turk-Browne, "Hippocampal encoding of memories in human infants", Science 387:1316-1320 (2025), Yale: awake fMRI of 26 infants aged about 4-25 months; hippocampal activity while seeing an image predicted later memory, stronger after 12 months; the authors suggest infantile amnesia may be a retrieval problem (how long the memories last is unknown).
