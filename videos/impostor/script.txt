You get the job. You get the praise. And a quiet voice in your head says: they're going to find out. You're not as good as they think. Sooner or later, somebody will notice. Now listen to this. In 2018, Michelle Obama sat in front of a sold-out hall in London and said: I still have a little impostor syndrome. It never goes away. Tom Hanks, with two Oscars at home, said it on the radio: When are they going to discover that I am, in fact, a fraud? That feeling has a name. In 1978, two psychologists, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, described it in more than a hundred and fifty successful women. Top grades. Top jobs. And each one believed she had fooled everybody. Every time they succeeded, they blamed it on luck, or hard work, or charm. Anything except being good. They called it the impostor phenomenon. But here's the twist. They thought it was mostly women. Then in 2020, a review of sixty-two studies found men feel it just as much. So that voice isn't proof that you're a fraud. Michelle Obama hears it too. And next time someone praises your work, do what those two psychologists asked the women to do. Write it down. And notice how fast you try to explain it away.
