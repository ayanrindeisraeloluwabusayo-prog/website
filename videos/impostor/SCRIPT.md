# Why do you feel like a fraud, even when you are doing well? (impostor syndrome)

Script style: **Full Blend** (see video-styles/script-styles.md). same script style as before (short lines, opens on the viewer), then a real,
checked moment from famous people, then the science. Draft for the owner's approval.

## Script (Full Blend, final as recorded)

You get the job. You get the praise. And a quiet voice in your head says: they're going to find out. Now listen to this. In 2018, Michelle Obama sat in front of a sold-out hall in London and said: I still have a little impostor syndrome. It never goes away. And Tom Hanks, with two Oscars at home, said it on the radio: When are they going to discover that I am, in fact, a fraud? Now, that feeling got its name back in 1978, when two psychologists, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, kept meeting the same kind of woman in therapy and in their classes, more than a hundred and fifty of them, women with top grades and top jobs, and every one was quietly sure she had fooled everybody. And you see, whenever one of them succeeded, she blamed it on luck, or hard work, or charm, anything except actually being good, so they called it the impostor phenomenon. But here's the twist: they thought it was mostly women, and then in 2020, a review of sixty-two studies found that men feel it just as much. So that voice isn't proof that you're a fraud, because Michelle Obama hears it too. And next time someone praises your work, write it down, and notice how fast you try to explain it away.

## Facts and where each comes from
- Michelle Obama, Royal Festival Hall (Southbank Centre), London, 3 December 2018, in conversation with Chimamanda Ngozi
  Adichie: "I still have a little impostor syndrome, it never goes away." (BBC, CBS News, Quartz and others.)
- Tom Hanks, NPR Fresh Air, 26 April 2016: "When are they going to discover that I am, in fact, a fraud?" He has two
  Academy Awards (Philadelphia, Forrest Gump).
- Clance, P. R., & Imes, S. A. (1978). The imposter phenomenon in high achieving women. Psychotherapy: Theory, Research
  & Practice. Based on their work with more than 150 high-achieving women, who put success down to luck, effort or charm
  and believed they had fooled others. It was built from therapy and teaching work, not a controlled experiment, so the
  script says "described", not "proved".
- Bravata et al. (2020). Prevalence, predictors, and treatment of impostor syndrome: a systematic review. Journal of
  General Internal Medicine, 35(4), 1252-1275. 62 studies; rates from 9 % to 82 % depending on the test used; men and women
  affected at similar rates.
- The closing tip comes from the 1978 paper: the women were asked to keep a record of the positive feedback they
  received and of how they stopped themselves accepting it. It is offered as something to try, not as a proven cure
  (the 2020 review found treatments have been little studied).
- Nothing is diagnosed: both famous people are quoted in their own public words only.
