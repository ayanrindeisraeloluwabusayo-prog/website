# The more people who see you in trouble, the less likely anyone is to help

## Script (~200 words, about 63 s)

The more people who see you in trouble, the less likely any one of them is to help.

It started with a murder. New York, 1964. A young woman called Kitty Genovese was attacked outside her home.
Two weeks later, a newspaper reported that thirty-eight people watched, and nobody called the police.

The story shocked the world. So two psychologists tested it.
They put students in separate rooms, talking over an intercom. Then one voice began to have a seizure.
When students thought they were the only one listening, eighty-five percent rushed to get help.
When they thought four others could hear it too, only thirty-one percent did.
Everyone assumed someone else would act. They called it the bystander effect.

But here's the twist. The story that started it all was wrong.
There were never thirty-eight silent witnesses. Only a handful saw anything clearly.
One neighbour shouted, and scared the attacker away. Another ran down and held Kitty until help came.
In 2016, the same newspaper admitted its story was grossly exaggerated.

So the effect is real. But the legend isn't.
And if you ever need help in a crowd, don't shout at everyone. Point at one person.

## Sources and what each supports
- Darley, J. M., & Latané, B. (1968). Bystander intervention in emergencies: Diffusion of responsibility. Journal of
  Personality and Social Psychology, 8, 377-383. Students in separate rooms on an intercom; a recorded voice has a
  seizure. Helped before the fit ended: 85 % when alone with the victim, 62 % with one other listener, 31 % with four
  other listeners.
- Manning, R., Levine, M., & Collins, A. (2007). The Kitty Genovese murder and the social psychology of helping: The
  parable of the 38 witnesses. American Psychologist, 62, 555-562. No evidence for 38 watching witnesses; the
  prosecutor found about half a dozen who saw anything; a witness shouted and the attacker left the first time;
  Sophia Farrar, a neighbour and friend, held her before help arrived.
- The New York Times, 2016 (obituary of the killer): the 1964 article "grossly exaggerated the number of witnesses
  and what they had perceived". It also says some neighbours did ignore cries for help, so the script says the
  legend was exaggerated, not that everyone helped.
