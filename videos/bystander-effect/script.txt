You collapse on a busy street. Dozens of people see you. And nobody stops. The more people who see you in trouble, the less likely any one of them is to help. It started with a murder. New York, 1964. A young woman called Kitty Genovese was attacked outside her home. Two weeks later, a newspaper reported that thirty-eight people watched, and nobody called the police. The story shocked the world. So two psychologists tested it. They put students in separate rooms, talking over an intercom. Then one voice began to have a seizure. When students thought they were the only one listening, eighty-five percent rushed to get help. When they thought four others could hear it too, only thirty-one percent did. Everyone assumed someone else would act. They called it the bystander effect. But here's the twist. The story that started it all was wrong. There were never thirty-eight silent witnesses. Only a handful saw anything clearly. One neighbour shouted, and scared the attacker away. Another ran down and held Kitty until help came. In 2016, the same newspaper admitted its story was grossly exaggerated. So the effect is real. But the legend isn't. And if you ever need help in a crowd, don't shout at everyone. Point at one person.
