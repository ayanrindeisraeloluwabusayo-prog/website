# Why you look better in the mirror than in photos (Full Blend, v1, waiting for approval)

You check the mirror. You look good. Then someone takes a photo, and you think: is that really me? Remember when Apple added a switch to iPhones in 2020 that saves your selfies flipped, the way the mirror shows you? People had been asking for it for years. And you see, back in 1977, three American psychologists photographed women students and printed each picture two ways: normal, and flipped like a mirror. The women mostly preferred the flipped version of themselves. Their close friends preferred the normal one. It's called the mere exposure effect: we like what we see most often. You've seen your mirror face thousands of times, and no face is perfectly even, so a photo shows you a slightly unfamiliar version of yourself. But here's the twist: the camera can change your face too. In 2018, researchers at Rutgers and Stanford worked out that a selfie taken about a foot away makes the base of your nose look about thirty percent wider than a photo taken from five feet. And in a 2008 study at the University of Chicago, people picked a version of their face that had been made more attractive as their real face. So a bad photo isn't proof you look bad. It's a view you're not used to. Next time, flip the photo, step back, and let someone shoot from a few feet away.

## Sources (checked)
- Apple iOS 14 (2020) added the "Mirror Front Camera" setting, which saves front-camera photos as the mirrored preview.
- Mita, Dermer & Knight, "Reversed facial images and the mere-exposure hypothesis", JPSP 35(8):597-601 (1977): women preferred mirror prints of themselves; their friends preferred the true prints.
- Ward, Ward, Fried & Paskhover, "Nasal distortion in short-distance photographs: the selfie effect", JAMA Facial Plastic Surgery (2018), Rutgers and Stanford: at about 12 inches the nasal base looks about 30% wider than at 5 feet.
- Epley & Whitchurch, "Mirror, mirror on the wall: enhancement in self-recognition", Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin (2008), University of Chicago: people picked an attractively enhanced morph as their own face.
