She leaves a full sack of grated cassava sitting in a corner for three whole days. You see, in this compound, garri is money. Whoever reaches the market first on Friday sells first. Her husband's family grate, press and fry on the same day. By evening their basins are full. Hers? One sack, tied tight, under a heavy stone. Her mother-in-law doesn't whisper it. "This one is not making garri. She is raising it." Every market day they sell out before noon. She arrives on day three, when the customers have gone home. Her garri is sour. Theirs is quick. The whole compound agrees: lazy. Then the rains fail. One month. Two. The yams are gone. The maize is gone. Only the bitter cassava survives, so everybody eats garri morning and night, and the family rushes it faster than ever. First it's her brother-in-law. His knees go stiff. Then the children start walking on their toes. Then one morning her mother-in-law cannot stand up from her stool. You see, cassava carries a hidden poison: cyanide. Grating breaks it loose, but it needs time to leave, into the air and into the water squeezed out of that sack. And bitter cassava in a drought carries the most. In 1981, in Mozambique, a drought and rushed cassava left more than a thousand people unable to walk. So that sour sack in the corner was never laziness. It was the only safe garri in the compound.
