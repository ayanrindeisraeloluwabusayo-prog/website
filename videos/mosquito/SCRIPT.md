# Why mosquitoes bite you and not the person next to you (Full Blend, approved)

You're sitting outside with a friend. Same chair, same air, same night. You get five bites. They get none. Remember when Bill Gates stood on stage at a TED conference in 2009, opened a jar, and let mosquitoes fly out into the audience? He was talking about malaria, and he said poor people shouldn't be the only ones to experience it. The mosquitoes weren't carrying any disease, but the point landed. Now, the first thing a mosquito follows is your breath. It tracks the carbon dioxide you breathe out from far away, and you see, that's why a study in The Gambia in 2000 found that pregnant women attracted twice as many malaria mosquitoes as other women: they breathed out about a fifth more air, and their bellies were a little warmer. But here's the twist: it's not your sweet blood. It's your skin. In 2022, scientists at Rockefeller University had dozens of volunteers wear nylon stockings on their arms, then let mosquitoes choose between them, over and over, for years. The favourite people had much more of certain natural acids on their skin, and they stayed mosquito magnets year after year. So Bill Gates was right to make his audience feel it. Mosquitoes don't bite at random. If you're the one who always gets bitten, it isn't bad luck. It's your skin. Use repellent, sleep under a net, and let your friend laugh. The mosquitoes have already made their choice.
