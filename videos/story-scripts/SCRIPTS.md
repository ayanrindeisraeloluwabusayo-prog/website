# Story scripts for the cartoon shorts (drafts for approval)

Written to the owner's six-beat formula (`.claude/skills/cartoon-3d-short/OWNER-STORY-PLAYBOOK.md`): weird action,
stakes, a doubter mocks them, they lose at first, "You see…", payoff and stop. Present tense, about 215-250 words
each (60-65 s at the reference pace). Voice: Brian.

In every script the people and their lines are a made-up story (no real person is quoted). The science and the
history under "Facts" are real and sourced. Anything not confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed".

---

## 1. The lazy wife's garri

She leaves a full sack of grated cassava sitting in a corner for three whole days.

You see, in this compound, garri is money. Whoever reaches the market first on Friday sells first.

Her husband's family grate, press and fry on the same day. By evening their basins are full. Hers? One sack, tied
tight, under a heavy stone.

Her mother-in-law doesn't whisper it. "This one is not making garri. She is raising it."

Every market day they sell out before noon. She arrives on day three, when the customers have gone home. Her garri
is sour. Theirs is quick. The whole compound agrees: lazy.

Then the rains fail. One month. Two. The yams are gone. The maize is gone. Only the bitter cassava survives, so
everybody eats garri morning and night, and the family rushes it faster than ever.

First it's her brother-in-law. His knees go stiff. Then the children start walking on their toes. Then one morning
her mother-in-law cannot stand up from her stool.

You see, cassava carries a hidden poison: cyanide. Grating breaks it loose, but it needs time to leave, into the
air and into the water squeezed out of that sack. And bitter cassava in a drought carries the most.

In 1981, in Mozambique, a drought and rushed cassava left more than a thousand people unable to walk.

So that sour sack in the corner was never laziness. It was the only safe garri in the compound.

**Facts**
- Cassava holds compounds that release cyanide. Grating lets the plant's own enzyme break them down, and the cyanide
  then escapes as gas and in the pressed-out water. Traditional garri is pressed and left 3-4 days.
  ([Codex code of practice](https://www.gov.si/assets/organi-v-sestavi/UVHVVR/Varna-hrana/Onesnazevala-v-zivilih/CXP_073e1.pdf))
- Drought raises the cyanide in bitter cassava, and shortcuts in processing during food shortage cause konzo, a
  sudden permanent stiffness of the legs. The 1981 outbreak in Nampula, Mozambique had over 1,000 cases.
  ([Nzwalo and Cliff, PLoS NTD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3125150), [Cliff et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20654676/))

**Honest notes**
- Same-day garri is not always dangerous: studies show well-grated, well-pressed mash can be safe within a day. The
  danger is bitter cassava, a drought, shortcuts and nothing else to eat, all together. The script says exactly that.
- In Mozambique the cassava was mostly sun-dried flour, not garri. The script says "rushed cassava", which is true.
- The sour taste comes from the waiting (fermentation). The script does not say the smell is the poison.

## 2. The woman who watered her sand

She puts her tomatoes inside a clay pot, puts that pot inside a bigger pot, and pours water on the sand between
them.

You see, it is April in Jigawa. Forty degrees by noon. A tomato picked this morning is soft by tomorrow evening, so
every seller in this market lives by one rule: sell it today, or throw it tomorrow.

The woman at the next stall watches her pouring water on sand. "Madam, are you selling tomatoes or planting them?"

The whole row laughs. And they have reason to. By four o'clock everybody is shouting half price, and she is the
only one not shouting. She lays a wet cloth over her pot and goes home with half her tomatoes unsold.

Day two, the others are picking out the rotten ones. Day three, they are tipping baskets into the gutter and
buying fresh. She lifts the wet cloth. Same tomatoes. Still firm. Still full price.

You see, you already know this trick. It is why water from a clay pot is cold. Water creeps through the clay and
dries in the hot wind, and drying steals heat, the same way you shiver stepping out of the bath. With wet sand
around it, the inside pot can sit fourteen degrees cooler than the market.

A teacher from this same north, Mohammed Bah Abba, built it in the 1990s, and it reached tens of thousands of
families.

So next week, when the row starts shouting half price, nobody is laughing at the woman who waters her sand. They
are asking where she bought the pot.

**Facts**
- Mohammed Bah Abba (1964-2010), a teacher from northern Nigeria, developed the pot-in-pot cooler in the 1990s and
  received a Rolex Award for Enterprise. Evaporation through the outer pot cools the inside by up to 14 °C. By 2005
  he had distributed about 91,800 pots. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Bah_Abba), [Rolex Awards list](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolex_Awards_for_Enterprise))

**Not confirmed**
- The often-repeated figures "eggplants 27 days instead of 3, tomatoes 3 weeks" could not be confirmed in this
  session, so the script only shows three days. The award year appears as 2000 in some places and 2001 in others,
  so the script gives no year.

## 3. The drum has no legs

He squeezes a drum under his arm and tells the trader, "Before you reach the next town, they will know your name."

You see, the trader has just sold the whole market short. Six yards of cloth, folded to look like eight. And his
plan is simple: by the time anyone measures, he will be selling in the next town, where nobody knows him.

So he laughs at the drummer. "Your drum, does it have legs? Can it ride a horse?"

And he is right to laugh. He has the fastest horse on the road. He leaves before noon, he doesn't stop to eat, and
behind him the drumming gets fainter, and fainter, and gone. Nobody passes him on the road. Nobody can.

He rides into the next town before sunset, smiling. Then he notices the market women. Arms folded. A small boy
points at him and says his name. And an old woman holds up a measuring stick. "Six yards," she says. "Not eight."

You see, in Yoruba the tune of a word is part of the word. Say it high, middle or low and it means something
else. The drummer squeezes the cords to tighten the skin, the drum climbs and drops like a voice, and it plays the
tune of a whole sentence. One drummer hears it and passes it on to the next.

The drum has no legs. It never needed them. It only had to be faster than a horse.

**Facts**
- Yoruba is a tonal language with three levels (low, mid, high). The hourglass talking drum changes pitch when the
  player squeezes its cords, and it copies the pitch and rhythm of speech, not the consonants and vowels.
  ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com:443/smart-news/how-west-african-drums-really-talk-180978296/), [Frontiers for Young Minds](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2022.755390/pdf), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_drum))

**Honest notes**
- The trader, the horse and the towns are a made-up tale, told as a tale. Listeners understand drum messages through
  well-known phrases and context, which is why the script has the drum send a short, simple message.
- No distance is claimed for how far one drum carries; the script uses a relay of drummers.

## 4. The dust on your table (a "you" story)

Every December you wipe the brown dust off your table, and by evening it is back.

You see, harmattan has one job, and it is to ruin your life. Your lips crack. Your white shirt turns brown on the
line. Your flight is cancelled because the pilot cannot see the runway.

So you fight it. You close every window. Your mother laughs. "You want to lock out harmattan? Lock out the sky as
well."

And she is right. It comes under the door. It comes through the net. You wipe the table in the morning, you wipe
it at noon, and at night you can still write your name on the television.

You lose. Everybody loses. Every single year.

You see, that dust is not from your street. Most of it comes from one place in Chad, the dried-up floor of a lake
that was once bigger than any lake alive today. That floor is made of the shells of tiny creatures that died there
thousands of years ago, and those shells are full of phosphorus, which is plant food.

The wind lifts it, and it does not stop at your house. It crosses Nigeria. It crosses the whole Atlantic. Every
year about twenty-seven million tons of it lands on the Amazon rainforest, which is losing its own plant food to
the rain, and that dust replaces it.

So the brown film on your table is not dirt. It is fertiliser for the biggest forest on Earth, and your house was
simply in the way.

**Facts**
- NASA satellite data (2007-2013): about 27.7 million tons of Saharan dust settle on the Amazon each year, carrying
  about 22,000 tons of phosphorus, roughly what the forest loses to rain and flooding. The Bodélé Depression in Chad,
  an ancient lake bed of dead micro-organisms, is a key source.
  ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/nasa-satellite-reveals-how-much-saharan-dust-feeds-amazons-plants/), [Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2015/03/how-the-sahara-keeps-the-amazon-rainforest-going/))

**Not confirmed in this session**
- "Most" of Nigeria's harmattan dust coming from the Bodélé, and "bigger than any lake alive today" for the old Lake
  Chad. Both are widely reported; I would check them before recording. The NASA team also says the amount changes a
  lot from year to year.

## 5. Why the mosquitoes choose you (a "you" story)

Two of you sleep in the same room, and every morning you have twelve bites and your roommate has none.

You see, this is war now. Same room, same window, same hole in the net. And he wakes up smooth like a baby.

He has an answer, of course. "Your blood is sweet. It is all that sugar you drink."

So you stop the sugar. Fourteen bites. You change your soap. Eleven bites. You sleep in socks and a hoodie in the
heat, and they bite your face.

Then you get clever. You swap beds. He sleeps by the window, you sleep by the door. In the morning he stretches,
smiling. Not one bite. They flew past him to find you.

You see, in 2022 scientists in New York tested this. They made people wear nylon sleeves on their arms, then let
mosquitoes choose between the sleeves. The mosquitoes picked the same people, again and again, and months later
they still picked the same people.

It was never the blood. The people they loved had far more of certain acids on their skin, the oily kind your skin
makes by itself. Soap did not change it. Food did not change it.

So you can drop the sugar, the socks and the swapping of beds. Your skin is calling them, and it will not stop.

Which leaves one thing that works: the net with no hole in it.

**Facts**
- De Obaldia and colleagues (Vosshall lab, Rockefeller University), Cell, October 2022: volunteers wore nylon
  sleeves; the most attractive people had much higher levels of carboxylic acids on their skin, and this stayed
  stable for a year or more. ([NIH summary](https://nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/skin-compounds-associated-attractiveness-mosquitoes), [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/10/221027124001.htm))

**Honest notes**
- The study used the Aedes mosquito (dengue, yellow fever), not the malaria mosquito, and it shows a strong link, not
  final proof that the acids are the cause. The line "soap and food did not change it" comes from the researchers'
  comments and needs a check against the paper before recording. The "100 times more attractive" figure from news
  reports is left out.
