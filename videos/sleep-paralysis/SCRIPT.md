# Sleep paralysis: awake but unable to move (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

You wake up in the dark. Your eyes are open. But you can't move. You can't speak. And you feel like someone is in the room with you. Remember when Kendall Jenner told her family on Keeping Up with the Kardashians, "I wake up in the middle of the night and I can't move"? She was scared to fall asleep. And you see, it has a name: sleep paralysis. When you dream, your brain switches off most of your muscles, so you don't act out your dreams. Now, sometimes you wake up before that switch turns back on. Your mind is awake, but your body is still asleep, and pieces of the dream spill into the room: a shadow, a figure, a weight on your chest. In 2011, psychologists Brian Sharpless and Jacques Barber pulled together thirty-five studies with more than thirty-six thousand people. About eight in every hundred people have had it at least once. Among students, it was more than one in four. But here's the twist: what you believe about it can change how bad it gets. In Egypt, many people blame it on a jinn, a spirit. Researchers compared people there with people in Denmark, where most see it as a brain glitch. In Egypt, it was more common, and those who blamed spirits were more frightened and stayed frozen longer. So if it happens to you, you're not cursed, and you're not in danger. It's your brain waking up a little early. Next time, remind yourself it will pass, breathe slowly, and try to wiggle one finger or toe.

## Sources (checked)
- Kendall Jenner, Keeping Up with the Kardashians (E!, 6 Nov 2016): "I wake up in the middle of the night and I can't move"; she said she was scared to fall asleep (Fox News, Live Science, IBTimes coverage).
- REM sleep atonia: during dreaming (REM) sleep the brain blocks most muscle movement; sleep paralysis is waking while this continues, often with hallucinations (intruder, chest pressure). NHS: harmless, passes in seconds to minutes.
- Sharpless & Barber, "Lifetime prevalence rates of sleep paralysis: a systematic review", Sleep Medicine Reviews 15(5):311-315 (2011): 35 studies, 36,533 people; 7.6% general population, 28.3% students.
- Jalal & Hinton (2013), Culture, Medicine and Psychiatry; Jalal, Romanelli-Gobbi & Hinton (2014): Egypt vs Denmark; 44% vs 25% had experienced it; in Egypt, supernatural (jinn) beliefs linked with more fear and longer immobility (a link, not proven cause).
- Coping tips (stay calm, reappraise, slow breathing, small movement of a finger or toe) follow Jalal's suggested approach and common sleep-medicine advice.
