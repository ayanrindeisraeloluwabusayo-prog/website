A car swerves toward you. A glass slips off the table. And for a moment, everything moves in slow motion. You see every detail, like the world pressed pause. Remember when you nearly fell, and one second felt like ten? When the neuroscientist David Eagleman was eight years old, he fell off the roof of a house that was still being built. The fall felt like it lasted forever. Years later, he worked it out: a fall like that takes less than one second. And you see, in 2007, he decided to test it. His team dropped volunteers from a tower about thirty-one meters high, into a net. The fall took about three seconds. Afterwards, people guessed their own fall had lasted about a third longer than the falls they watched other people take. But here's the twist: on their wrists, they wore a little screen flashing numbers too fast to read. If time had really slowed down for them, they would have been able to read it during the fall. They couldn't. Time never slowed down. Your memory did. When you're scared, your brain records the moment in extra detail. And when you look back on all that detail, your brain decides it must have taken longer. So that slow-motion moment wasn't your brain seeing faster. It was your brain remembering harder. Next time a moment feels like it lasted forever, remember: it didn't. You just remembered every second of it.
