# Why time slows down in an accident (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

A car swerves toward you. A glass slips off the table. And for a moment, everything moves in slow motion. You see every detail, like the world pressed pause. Remember when you nearly fell, and one second felt like ten? When the neuroscientist David Eagleman was eight years old, he fell off the roof of a house that was still being built. The fall felt like it lasted forever. Years later, he worked it out: a fall like that takes less than one second. And you see, in 2007, he decided to test it. His team dropped volunteers from a tower about thirty-one metres high, into a net. The fall took about three seconds. Afterwards, people guessed their own fall had lasted about a third longer than the falls they watched other people take. But here's the twist: on their wrists, they wore a little screen flashing numbers too fast to read. If time had really slowed down for them, they would have been able to read it during the fall. They couldn't. Time never slowed down. Your memory did. When you're scared, your brain records the moment in extra detail. And when you look back on all that detail, your brain decides it must have taken longer. So that slow-motion moment wasn't your brain seeing faster. It was your brain remembering harder. Next time a moment feels like it lasted forever, remember: it didn't. You just remembered every second of it.

## Sources (checked)
- David Eagleman's childhood fall: at about eight he fell from the roof of a house under construction; it felt slow; he later worked out a fall of about 12 feet takes about 0.86 s (NPR, "Why a brush with death triggers the slow-mo effect", 2010).
- Stetson, Fiesta & Eagleman, "Does time really slow down during a frightening event?", PLoS ONE 2(12): e1295 (2007): volunteers free-fell about 31 m (about 150 ft) into a net, a fall of about 2.5-3 s; they recalled their own fall as 36% longer than others' falls; a wrist "perceptual chronometer" flashed numbers they could not read at faster-than-normal speed during the fall, so perception did not speed up; the effect is in recollection (frightening events lay down richer, denser memories).
