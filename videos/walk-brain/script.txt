You're stuck on a problem. You get up, go for a walk, and halfway down the street, the answer just appears. Remember when Charles Darwin walked the same path behind his house, round and round, every single day? He piled up flints at the turn and knocked one away each lap, so he could keep thinking without counting. And you see, there's a reason it works. In 2014, Stanford psychologists Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz asked people to come up with new uses for everyday objects, once sitting and once walking. In one experiment, walking boosted their creative ideas by about sixty percent. Walking also changes your brain over time. In 2011, in a study led by Kirk Erickson at the University of Pittsburgh, older adults who walked forty minutes, three times a week, for a year, grew their hippocampus, the brain's memory centre, by about two percent. That's like turning back one to two years of ageing. But here's the twist: it isn't the view. The Stanford walkers who got the boost were on a treadmill, facing a blank wall. Just moving your legs was enough. And if you can, choose green. In 2015, a ninety-minute walk in nature quietened a brain area linked to dark, repetitive thoughts, while a city walk did not. So when you're stuck, stop staring at the screen. Do what Darwin did. Get up, walk, and let your brain do the rest.
