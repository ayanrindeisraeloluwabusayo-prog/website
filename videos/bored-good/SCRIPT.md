# Why being bored is good for you (Full Blend, v1, approved; thumbnail C)

You're stuck in a queue. No phone. Nothing to do. Every minute feels like an hour. Remember when J.K. Rowling was stuck on a delayed train from Manchester to London in 1990? Her pen didn't work, so she just sat there and thought. And on that train, she has said, the idea for Harry Potter came to her. And you see, in 2014, psychologists Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman, at the University of Central Lancashire in England, put boredom to the test. They gave people one of the dullest jobs they could think of: copying numbers out of a phone book. Then everyone had to come up with as many uses as they could for a pair of plastic cups. The bored group came up with more ideas than people who skipped the boring job. But here's the twist: the more boring it got, the better. In a second study, some people didn't even write the numbers down. They just read them. And they came up with the most ideas of all. The researchers think that when a task is too dull to hold your attention, your mind starts to wander, and wandering is where new ideas come from. So boredom isn't wasted time. It's your brain making room to think. Next time you're bored, don't grab your phone straight away. Sit with it for ten minutes, like Rowling on that train, and see what shows up.

## Sources (checked)
- J.K. Rowling: the idea came on a delayed Manchester-to-London train in 1990; she had no working pen and "simply sat and thought" (her official biography; her 2020 post confirming the Manchester-to-London train).
- Mann & Cadman, "Does being bored make us more creative?", Creativity Research Journal 26(2):165-173 (2014), University of Central Lancashire: copying phone numbers, then uses for plastic cups; the bored group gave more uses than controls; in study 2 the reading-only group gave the most; the authors link this to daydreaming.
